# ABSTRACT:
Acute-on-chronic diseases are marked by progressive, often clinically silent, deterioration bifurcated by sudden shifts in compensatory state. Alcohol-associated hepatitis is an acute failure that can occur on chronic alcohol-associated liver disease. How the shift of metabolic pathway functions sum to failure of the whole system during this shift from chronic to acute phases is not well understood. To understand this decompensation, we developed a mathematical framework that coarse-grains hepatocyte metabolism into modules, each representing a metabolic cycles. Modules are coupled through shared metabolites and energy costs. Each module operates at a velocity (metabolic flux), the rate at which material moves through its biochemical reactions. To capture how the coupled network responds to perturbations such as ethanol, we introduce a system-wide constraint grounded in resource sharing. Because modules draw on a common, limited pool of metabolites and energy, their velocities cannot vary independently: the total velocity across modules is conserved, and the stoichiometry of the shared resources determines how it is distributed among them. Therefore, perturbation cannot create capacity, only reallocate it. Implementing this framework, we simulated a range of liver states under ethanol perturbation corresponding to healthy and diseased parameters. We were able to re-capture $6$ distinct clinical scenarios including that of corticosteroid treatment. Under simulated corticosteroid treatment, severe AH presentations responded in 72–82% of cases, a range that brackets the clinically observed response rate of 75% . This agreement was not tuned into the model; it is an emergent result of the network coupling, and results from a range of metabolic parameters. The simulations also suggest that treatment may be destabilizing for trajectories with milder metabolic decompensation. More broadly, this framework provides a general, mechanistic way to characterize the landscape of how and when metabolic compensation fails in acute-on-chronic system failure.
